# E85 and ethanol blend profile

## Claim
E85 needs about 1.5 times the fuel mass of gasoline (about 35 to 40 % more volume). Its knock resistance and cooling usually give substantial margin, which pays off where the engine was knock-limited on pump fuel, and the actual ethanol content varies. It does not make an engine immune to knock, it is hard to start cold, and it absorbs water.

## Why (derivation)
See the stoichiometry and energy entries. Cold-start needs extra fuel because ethanol evaporates poorly at low temperature. Three properties change the required fuel: the stoichiometric ratio (about 9.8 for E85 against 14.7), density (E85 is heavier per volume) and viscosity, which alters injector volume flow at the same pressure and pulse by an injector-specific amount. Ethanol burns faster than gasoline, so in regions that were not knock-limited on gasoline MBT moves slightly later; in regions that were knock-limited, the extra margin allows far more timing or boost.

## Assumptions
The blend is measured or known.

## Breaks when
- Seasonal blends can be well below 85 percent ethanol. Fuel system capacity is the first thing to fail.
- Very high boost engines on alcohol are more prone to pre-ignition than to knock (see `principles/combustion/knock`).
- Very rich E85 (for example 0.76 at high boost) is hard to ignite and risks misfire unless the ignition is strong.
- Left standing, E85 absorbs water in humid climates and can leave gel-like residue; ferrous injector internals can rust and stick. Failures one practitioner saw were on alcohol after weeks of standing, not on gasoline.

## Practical use
- Check injector duty and pump capacity first. Treat a move to E85 as a new tune, not a fuel table scale. Multiply gasoline injector and pump requirements by about 1.4, and check every component's ethanol compatibility.
- In a model-based ECU, enter the blend's stoichiometric ratio, density, its temperature coefficient and molar mass, leave the VE table alone, start on the new fuel and compare closed-loop trim with the pump-fuel trims. A uniform offset is probably viscosity; scale the whole table. This only works if the pump tune had every parameter and the injector data right.
- Fit a content sensor even on a dedicated E85 car and set the fuel properties to the measured content; a mismatch between content and tune is a common cause of damage.
- Lambda targets: one practitioner uses the same as gasoline (around 0.80 on turbo engines at full load); some tuners run slightly leaner (about 0.82) because of the cooling. A target of 0.78 to 0.80 is preferred over very rich values at high boost; if a richer target shows no misfire or power loss it is simply extra margin.
- Timing: optimise on the dyno. Expect a little less timing (a degree or two) in light-load regions that were at MBT on gasoline, and large gains where gasoline was knock-limited. Keep audio knock monitoring running; many engines reach MBT on E85, but not all. Raise a knock threshold carried over from a pump tune only after confirming by ear that the extra crossings are noise from the higher combustion pressure.
- Boost: on many factory turbo engines the pump-fuel knock ceiling disappears, and turbo flow, back pressure or engine strength becomes the limit. Engines built for E85 can run compression that would be knock-limiting on pump fuel (practitioners cite 14 to 15:1 NA and 10 to 11:1 boosted).
- Cold start: add fuel overall, proportionally more in cranking than warm-up. Near-stock starts are achievable down to about 5 C in one practitioner's experience; below about 0 to 5 C true E85 can be close to impossible, which is why winter blends carry more gasoline. Once warm, idle control and acceleration enrichment usually need no change.
- Storage: one practitioner drains E85 and fills with gasoline if a car will sit more than about a week.
- Ethanol content: most of the knock benefit is reached by about E30 to E40; practitioners saw almost no further power from E70 or E85 to E100, so E30 to E40 gives good gains without a full fuel system rework.

## Worked example
An injector at 60 % duty on gasoline is near 86 % on E85 at the same lambda.
A turbo high-compression flat-four went from 181 to 226 kW at the wheels on E85, with boost raised from about 9 to 10 psi and about 6 degrees more top-end timing (21 to 22 degrees), still free of knock. Most of the gain came from timing; boost was limited by turbine back pressure on a small turbo. In a sweep test on a similar engine at 2500 rpm: at 60 kPa (not knock-limited) MBT was 32.7 degrees on gasoline and 30.6 on E85 for the same torque; at 120 kPa gasoline knocked at about 20 degrees while E85 reached MBT at 24.2 degrees with about 55 Nm more.

## Disputed / conflicts
- **E85 and knock immunity.** Some NA engines on E85 or race fuel cannot be made to knock at all, and then the engine is tuned to MBT. Other engines, notably high-compression ones at moderate boost, stay knock-limited on E85. Only testing the engine shows which.
- **Lambda targets.** Same as gasoline (one practitioner) or slightly leaner (other tuners); the leaner case relies on charge cooling and is not tested in the sources.
- **Winter blend content.** Practitioners' recollections of the minimum ethanol in winter pump E85 differ (about 55 % to 65 %). Treat them as unverified; measure content.
- **Race-blend E85.** A race E85 with oxygenate additives was reported to make about 4 to 8 % more power than pump E85; such additives can be incompatible with some injector seats.
- **Adding water/methanol to E85.** Expected to add little; untested in the sources.
- **Higher ethanol content.** A very high-compression, very knock-sensitive engine might still gain slightly above E85 (one practitioner's caveat).
